01 / Independent thesis

GhostMesh analysis, October 22, 2026. This film is an artwork informed by multi-agent mathematics. Source claims, observed frames, external research and proposed tests play different roles.

What we know: sampled frames and creator text. What we do not know: the render pipeline, soundtrack content, continuous trajectories, or a viewer's response.
Read the source limits

The pattern catches us before the explanation does.

At the 00:00 sample, a small group of luminous, ringed forms sits in a wide dark field. By the 04:40 sample, separated frames show denser cyan and pink points and repeated motifs. The scale of a cinema screen may make the later fields feel enveloping. But our samples cannot tell us how any individual point traveled between them. A constellation in a still is not a trajectory.

That distinction is part of the film's appeal, not a flaw in it. A viewer is invited to complete motion, depth and collective intent from arranged light. HORTRAME's description calls this a journey of thousands of simulated drones with a shared rhythm. The frames we inspected support a narrower observation: recurring luminous objects and changing compositions. The number of agents, their update law and the production pipeline are creator assertions until matched to code and render evidence.

GhostMesh's thesis: agreement is a rule for updating state; purpose is a meaning the composition can make us see. Neither should be borrowed as proof of the other.

From rows to rings, the camera teaches a grammar.

The 09:20 sample shows rows of small cross-like points. At 11:20, a darker center and concentric arrangement suggest an iris or an orbit. At 16:40, a dotted cube-like frame appears. Those words describe shapes visible at sampled instants. They do not identify an attractor, a graph topology, or a physical coordinate system. The film can place a viewer inside a mathematical mood without exposing the variables that drove a render.

A useful visual question is whether a change in what we perceive comes from agent states, camera movement, editing, brightness or scale. Sparse stills cannot apportion the cause. This is why a film still and an implementation trace must be treated as different evidence. Their agreement would be informative; one cannot substitute for the other.

What the priority-state mathematics actually offers.

HORTRAME says the film draws from Guillaume Bolduc's multi-agent control research: each agent has a probability row expressing priorities across active objectives, and neighboring rows can be mixed. The 13:00 visual sample only shows another arrangement of points; it does not reveal those rows. The Bolduc v07 preprint gives a more precise and more limited result. Under a specified arrival, mixing and departure history, it characterizes the returned population mean and when a newcomer priority restores the earlier endpoint. It also gives a counterexample to the idea that every round trip preserves that endpoint.

That matters because “the same agents came back” is not enough to guarantee “the same collective preference came back.” Removing an agent removes an objective coordinate and conditions each surviving row. The past interaction can therefore matter after the population returns to its earlier size. In a particular fixed final epoch, ordinary average consensus gives an endpoint interpretation. The paper calls its construction an offline input-level characterization, not an online distributed arrival protocol; it claims neither persistently open convergence nor physical rendezvous. Its companion browser motion aims at a precomputed epoch mean rather than a target discovered by local rows.

The separately cited Blondin and Hale optimization paper proves results for its own stated model. The Naderi, Blondin and Roy delay preprint analyzes delayed neighbor information under fixed membership and reports its own robot tests. Neither result transfers automatically to a film combining changing membership, delay, visual composition and music. The current arXiv record lists those three authors; a pending fourth-author credit in the creator description is not a current publication fact.

A bright circle cannot certify a control plane.

At the 18:20 sample, colored points surround a small dark center. By the 25:00 sample, a white-rimmed dark circle dominates several frames. The image evokes attraction, an eclipse or a portal. None is evidence of gravity, a singularity, or successful decentralized control. HORTRAME itself calls the black hole and cosmic setting artistic elements.

The distinction has a practical GhostMesh consequence. A system can have a valid theorem about a finite set of state transformations, a simulation that illustrates some parameters, and a striking render. It may still lack a protocol for unannounced departures, packet loss, disagreeing membership lists, label reuse, or recovery after an interrupted event. The v07 manuscript explicitly separates those obligations. A beautiful converging image invites trust; an operational system earns it through versioned code, explicit assumptions, adverse cases and readback after a transition.

This is not an accusation about the artwork. The film is free to use a composed trajectory. The boundary only matters when someone treats that trajectory as proof that a robot fleet or software-agent collective would behave the same way.

The pull of the image is plausible; its effect is unmeasured.

The 28:00 sample places a bright rim against a wide dotted plane or halo before the black ending samples. Contrast, apparent expansion and viewpoint changes can plausibly contribute to visual self-motion for some viewers. Research on visual self-motion and vection onset supports mechanisms under studied conditions, not an outcome for this film or its audience. Responses depend on display, field of view, visual sequence and person.

The creator describes pulse-coupled light and an adaptive musical score. Mirollo and Strogatz's 1990 oscillator paper concerns an idealized model, not verification of this production. We did not inspect the soundtrack or compare audio with image timing. A separate VR experiment suggests music can affect urge to move while beat alignment did not add vection strength in its brief trials. That counterpoint cautions against turning a plausible sensory design into a universal synchronization or trance claim. No medical or hypnosis effect is claimed here.

To test the claim, separate state, render and response.

We would start with a pinned implementation and a small, reproducible agent population. Record every priority row, membership event, mixing matrix, delay assumption and physical reference target. Compare a specified join–mix–depart history against the preprint's endpoint calculation; then change departure time, delay, message loss and membership labels one at a time. The predicted result is not that every history restores agreement. A counterexample should be visible in the state trace, and any recovery rule should state what it requires.

Then render the same trace with controlled camera and light changes, and render different traces with matched visual treatment. If viewers draw the same story from different states, the composition is doing consequential interpretive work. A consented viewer study could compare perceived motion under matched display conditions, but this Reader has not run one. A sound-shift or mute condition would require actual audio inspection and a separate study; it is not evidence we possess.

The experiment would leave the film's artistic accomplishment intact. It would tell us which part came from a state rule, which from production, and which from the observer. That is the useful GhostMesh lesson: keep the wonder, and keep the receipts for any operational claim.
