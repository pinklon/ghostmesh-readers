Full deconstruction

What Nous changed

Goldie’s walkthrough is useful because the comparison has a defined starting point. Arete is a post-trained Qwen3.8-27B checkpoint, with the same basic architecture. Nous reports supervised training on text with answers informed by its chosen teacher, Inkling. It trained the language-model weights and left the vision encoder frozen. The public weights make the result inspectable. Their availability alone says little about how the checkpoint behaves on a team’s actual work.

Nous built the intervention around a specific failure mode it observed in the base model. That gives us a tractable question to test. If an answer leaves out a documented fact or refuses a legitimate question, changing that behavior can improve the answer. The harder judgment is how the evaluation handles competing interpretations when the facts themselves are agreed.

What the 22% to 0.9% result means

Nous reports two separate outcomes on 803 English-language prompts. With thinking disabled and temperature set to zero for both checkpoints, average factual scores rose from 2.32 to 2.98 out of five. The share of responses that its judge flagged under the Chinese state-framing rubric fell from 22% to 0.9%. The published table averages five runs for these rows. It lists the thinking-on Arete result separately as provisional, and some other models in the table used different settings.

The factual score rewards coverage of required facts and assigns zero when an answer contradicts one. The framing score asks whether the answer adopts or endorses a particular official account under the rubric. A lower framing score therefore has a narrow interpretation. It tells you how often this judge applied that label on this set. A reader can value the improvement and still ask whether the rubric would be equally demanding with a different government, teacher, or topic.

The best defense of Nous’s design is that the prompts, source anchors and grading materials are available. The lab says its judge was calibrated against a small human-graded pilot and was blinded to model identity. That makes the comparison more useful than a collection of screenshots. It also makes it possible to test a different rubric against the same responses. These remain results produced by the lab that trained the model.

What to test before switching models

Nous reports near-equal results on its internal Hermes Bench and a three-point difference in Arete’s favor on its Terminal-Bench 2.1 run. The lab cautions about inference and harness differences when comparing its numbers with official results. These are useful checks on two measured workloads. A decision to swap models still depends on the workflows and failure costs of the team using it.

Run a small blind comparison before deploying either checkpoint for sensitive answers. Pin both model revisions and run the same prompts, context, decoding settings and tools. Include held-out questions about more than one government, questions where sources disagree, and ordinary tasks that could regress. Grade verifiable facts, unjustified refusals, unsupported assertions, and framing as separate outcomes. Ask human reviewers to adjudicate disagreements on a sample, then publish which settings and scoring choices changed the result.

The useful result would be a factual improvement that survives a different judge and a different topic mix, with ordinary task quality and safeguards holding up. If gains disappear under a second rubric or new errors appear on routine work, the original benchmark can still describe a real effect on its own test while giving us little basis to switch.
