Change the assignment before scaling the agent

Rieseberg's central move is to stop treating the first requested artifact as the real goal. A quarterly review is not valuable because it is a deck; it matters if people understand what changed and make a better decision. Jay's pitch example makes that accessible. The useful question is what outcome the deck was meant to produce, followed by whether a different artifact would help.

That shift becomes more credible in the depth segment. Rieseberg's original essay asks for raw data, professional methods, and an outside judge. In the video, Jay applies it to customer churn and site speed. A model claiming to be an expert is weak evidence; an inspectable analysis and an independent measurement are stronger. A second agent can offer another view, but a separate test or human review is often the clearer judge.

Range is more than assigning fictional roles. The essay says to borrow what another field actually does: its data, instruments, and ways of challenging conclusions. Jay's quarterly-review and real-estate examples show how changing the verb in a task can reveal those methods. Use extra perspectives when they might change the decision, not just to make the response longer.

Ambition changes the question. Evidence decides whether the answer improved.

The title and ranking need narrower readings

The title implies Anthropic shared a method. The traceable primary source is Rieseberg's personal post, written by someone who says he works on Claude. It is useful firsthand advice; it is not presented as an Anthropic-wide process. The essay lists four types including speed, and says its guidance is model-agnostic. Jay's three-part summary is a fair selection of the less obvious types, provided readers know what was omitted.

Jay repeatedly contrasts most users with the “top 1%.” The video does not supply a measured user distribution or show that these methods produce a particular percentile of performance. Its concrete examples are prompts and proposed workflows, not controlled outcome comparisons. Treat the percentile as framing. The promotional guide and skill are optional ways to package prompts; neither gives an agent data access, permission, or proof that a workflow succeeded.

The closing self-efficacy section is a reason to try harder questions, not proof that any ambitious goal is feasible. Jay's own final counterpoint is good: pace the ambition and know why you are doing the work. That purpose question belongs before an agent is allowed to act.

GhostMesh's takeaway: a bounded ambition trial

For one low-risk task, write a one-sentence outcome and an observable success measure. Then draft three prompts: altitude asks for the desired outcome rather than the first artifact; depth names the raw source, suitable methods, and an independent judge; range asks which neighboring field's method could expose a blind spot. Give every version the same permitted inputs and an explicit stop point.

Compare the outputs against the predefined measure and keep a record of the inputs, tool actions, cost, and corrections. If one frame improves the result, retain that method for similar scoped work. Any broader access or ongoing execution requires a separate human decision. This is a proposed GhostMesh experiment based on the essay's ideas, not an experiment performed by Jay or Rieseberg in the video.
