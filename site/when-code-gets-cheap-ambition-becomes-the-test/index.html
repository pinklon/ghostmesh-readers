When Code Gets Cheap, Ambition Becomes the Test

A cheaper model is not automatically a better system. But code-native agents can expand what a person attempts. The hard part moves toward evaluation, judgment, permission and accepted results.

Riley Brown with Suhail Doshi (Particle, Mixpanel) · 21 November 2026 · 60:49 · Original interview

GhostMesh assessment: A consequential and plausible transition hypothesis, not a 30× independently replicated benchmark or the death certificate of software engineering.

The larger claim behind the 30× headline

The interviewer comes with a familiar anxiety. He is paying for premium AI subscriptions, using increasingly capable models to edit videos, make 3D scenes and build applications, and wonders what happens when favorable plan economics stop resembling an unlimited resource. He describes one earlier month of API usage costing him $12,000. That anecdote is not a pricing survey; it is a sharp question about the economics of agent workloads. 08:04.

Suhail Doshi, whose present company Particle sells efficiency-oriented models and inference, gives a much more expansive answer. The number of tasks people can delegate may be growing faster than the price of each unit of intelligence is declining. Humans first asked questions in a chat window. Then they chained tool calls. Now agents are being assigned long trajectories. He envisages always-on cybersecurity agents and large concurrent groups of workers. These are possible demand drivers, not measured adoption forecasts. 09:15.

Later, the argument moves away from dollars. Interfaces built for human eyes and hands are not necessarily the best interfaces for software agents. Someone accustomed to managing a video timeline can now ask an agent to manipulate the underlying media and run FFmpeg. Someone accustomed to clicking through a 3D application can ask an agent to write the corresponding scripts. Doshi argues that the old separation between "coding tasks" and "work tasks" is losing explanatory power. 33:55.

Finally, the discussion reaches its most interesting proposition: new tools should enlarge what people try to accomplish, not simply compress the headcount or minutes attached to old tasks. The interviewer calls it scaling ambition. Doshi suggests that a person narrowly assigned to color grading might now aspire to develop an entire short film, provided work, capital and institutional constraints permit it. 51:12.

These are linked claims about the cost of intelligence, the structure of work, and the agency to choose a bigger problem. They should not be collapsed into a claim that expensive models are obsolete, software engineers have no future, or every creative project can now be accomplished by one person. The evidence does not establish those conclusions. The interview is most valuable as a hypothesis about where the bottleneck is moving.

GhostMesh verdict: The direction is plausible and consequential. The size, timing and universality of the transition remain unproved. The central question is not whether AI can produce artifacts more cheaply, but how an organization turns affordable capability into accepted work without losing control over truth, state and authority.

I. The falling price is real. The future bill is not settled.

It helps to distinguish four quantities that discussions of cheap intelligence routinely blend together.

Unit price is the charge for a token or a request under a stated price tier. Token efficiency is how many tokens a system produces while attempting a task; it may change with prompting, tools and training. Task efficiency includes retries, tool calls and time until a defined result is accepted. Total operating cost also includes human verification, rework, data movement, security, recovery and the cost of retaining an auditable artifact.

A useful, explicitly non-empirical accounting identity is:

Cost per accepted job = model/tool charges over all attempts + human verification and recovery + amortized infrastructure and governance costs.

This is not an invitation to assign arbitrary dollar values to everything. It is a reminder that a low token invoice does not automatically mean an inexpensive accepted result. Two models priced 30× apart can be much closer in total cost if the cheaper one needs repeated attempts and manual rescue. Conversely, reliable task-specific models can make an expensive default difficult to justify. Those are hypotheses to measure, not positions to take on brand loyalty.

Historical evidence supports declining benchmark-adjusted inference prices. Stanford's 2025 AI Index reported a reduction from $20 to $0.07 per million tokens for systems at a specified GPT-3.5-level MMLU threshold between late 2022 and October 2024, a reduction exceeding 280× on that specific comparison. That says something important about the industry trend. It does not say the cost of serving every difficult task has fallen by that factor, or that today's highest-capability models have become interchangeable. Stanford HAI.

Doshi's other observation matters just as much: demand can be elastic. A tool that used to perform a ten-turn exchange may perform hundreds of turns, perform background monitoring, generate and evaluate alternatives, or call other agents. Falling per-token prices and rising aggregate token expenditure can coexist. A 24/7 agent consumes time, power and tool resources even if it rarely delivers something worth accepting.

Subscription allowances are also not equivalent to public API pricing. The interview's $200-tier accounts and $12,000 API anecdote differ in workload, rate limits, timing and billing rules. They cannot establish the subsidy embedded in any particular plan. Doshi predicts that much future token expenditure will be enterprise-oriented, while consumers may be served at much lower apparent prices through other business models. That remains his projection, not a verified forecast. 08:04–16:20.

The practical design implication is subtle: a cheaper token may unlock valuable work that was previously unaffordable, but it also makes it economical to start much more work whose eventual value is uncertain. The decision and acceptance machinery becomes more important as the initiation cost falls.

II. Particle-1 is a concrete result, not a universal model verdict

The founder has a financial interest in this thesis. Particle sells model inference and seeks to make it more efficient. That interest does not invalidate the results, but it determines how the evidence should be weighted.

At approximately 18:57, Doshi describes a substantial improvement in token efficiency from post-training an open-weight DeepSeek-derived model. His public launch announcement, dated October 28, provides a more inspectable statement: Particle-1 is post-trained on DeepSeek V4.1 Flash using reinforcement learning to favor successful tasks with shorter trajectories. Across four named software/data benchmarks, Particle reports 28.4%–52.2% fewer output tokens and 34.4%–51.6% lower average cost per task relative to the base model. The company states that pass-at-one success rates fall within the base model's confidence intervals. Particle's own technical account.

The first published comparison supplies experimental conditions, including model pricing assumptions, a fixed cache-rate premise, rollout budgets and multiple attempts per task. These details are significantly stronger evidence than a casual "X percent better" slogan. They are still interested-party benchmark reports, not a GhostMesh replication or an independently established causal effect. A compression of output tokens can come from less unnecessary reasoning, fewer redundant calls, or prematurely omitted checks. Only a reliable external acceptance procedure can tell these apart.

The demonstration also raises a scientific measurement question. A model rewarded for fewer tokens may learn to stop unnecessary work, but it can also learn to under-report uncertainty or skip defensive verification. If success rates appear similar, inspect their intervals, the specific errors, the long-tail failures, differences in tool budgets, and the criteria defining task completion. Measure wall-clock latency and recovery, not merely token counts.

Even the figure being discussed should be pinned carefully: the interview's roughly 42% efficiency description and the blog's approximately 45% cross-task characterization are not interchangeable universal constants. The blog itself reports a broad range by benchmark. The right attribution is vendor-reported model behavior under specified benchmarks, not independently established global superiority.

Particle's business model contains a revealing tension. Around 23:00–24:30, Doshi describes monetizing tokens while trying to push their price toward zero. He sees analogies to payment processing and believes the market for low-cost intelligence is enormous. Commodity economics can support that wager if volume grows, operating efficiency improves, and the provider retains enough of the value chain. But margin compression, hardware constraints, switching costs, regulation, open competition and acquisition costs could also make the model precarious. Nothing in the interview settles Particle's eventual economics.

III. Five personal evaluations are a beginning, not a result

The most usable engineering instruction arrives at 30:07. Doshi suggests saving a handful of tasks someone actually performs, trying identical prompts across models, and comparing the outputs. For creative work, he imagines taking a successful editing trajectory and deriving a scoring exercise from it.

This is directionally better than purchasing the largest model because its name looks impressive. It also comes with limitations the interview itself acknowledges: evaluating a design, video or administrative deliverable can be tedious, and quality is not reducible to one obvious metric.

A folder containing five familiar prompts is a smoke test for local preferences. It is not a statistically persuasive capability ranking. It is sensitive to sampling, familiarity, grader bias, prompt selection, output order, unmeasured retries, overfitting to successful examples and changes in the model behind a provider alias. A frontier model used to create the grading rubric may encode the same stylistic preferences that make that model look good.

A stronger adoption test would proceed like this:

Dimension Minimal defensible measurement
Representative work Draw from actual workload strata, including easy, ambiguous, edge-case and failure-recovery tasks
Comparable conditions Freeze inputs, allowed tools, budgets, prompts, versions and acceptance criteria
Blind grading Separate creator preference from verifiable requirements; hide model identity when feasible
Success Report accepted outcomes and failure severity, not merely attractive-looking artifacts
Cost Count all attempts, token and tool charges, human review, repairs and elapsed time
Operations Track provenance, repeatability, security controls, data rights, logging and rollback
Uncertainty Use repeated paired runs, confidence intervals where appropriate, and clear limitations

For some tasks, deterministic tests or independent expert review are possible. For creative outputs, pair a stable rubric with blinded human comparison and measurable deliverable constraints. For regulated operations, record the test configuration, evidence source, reviewer and release authorization, not just a pleasing demo. The same model can be an excellent choice for one workload and a hazardous choice for another.

The result should be a routing policy rather than a global ranking. Use an inexpensive model for cases that regularly meet a defined threshold, escalate uncertainty or failures to a stronger model or human, and requalify when models, tools or source distributions change. A practical evaluator is therefore both a quality instrument and a procurement instrument.

This intersects with previous GhostMesh investigations but extends them: Reader #69 argued that completion cost is the right denominator; Reader #70 separated a narrow classifier victory from broader capability. Here the harder question is how ongoing, mixed-domain work becomes testable when many of the old user interfaces and work boundaries dissolve.

IV. Work can become code without becoming reliable software

The interview's strongest architectural insight begins around 34:29. A great deal of office and creative work consists of transforming structured files and invoking repeatable operations. Riley Brown says a model may edit video more effectively by manipulating files and running FFmpeg directly than by driving the interface of an editing application through an integration. Blender is scriptable through Python. Document production often has programmable paths too.

A plausible mechanism is training distribution. Text-and-code-trained models have encountered a large volume of command-line interfaces, libraries, scripts and their documented use, but far fewer examples of a particular application's graphical control state. Exposing the right deterministic API can be easier than teaching a model to click the right rectangle. This is a hypothesis about specific tools and models; the interview does not present a controlled FFmpeg-versus-editor experiment.

There is a real opportunity here. Code-native interfaces can bypass UI friction, make batch operations reproducible, and enable people who lack deep experience in a particular professional application to specify an outcome. The interface shifts from knowing a menu to describing desired constraints and accepting or rejecting a generated artifact.

But the analogy can also mislead. A video pipeline is not merely an input file and a command. Color fidelity, licensing, asset location, intermediate versions, render configuration, frame timing, audio sync, reproducibility and the ability to restore a previous state remain engineering concerns. A code-producing agent may satisfy the visible requirement and silently discard source files or produce a result nobody can reproduce.

The conversation recognizes the problem around 39:00: once the agent does the work headlessly, users may not know where files were saved or which version is authoritative. Doshi hopes models will learn to detect and propose retention or cleanup automatically. That could improve the interaction, but a model suggesting version control is not a substitute for a version-control system that actually retains bytes, provenance and rollback authority.

For an accountable implementation, distinguish four layers:

Intent: The request, acceptance criteria, allowed data and authority.

Execution: Scripts, environment, tools, resource ceilings and event logs.

Artifacts: Inputs, outputs, hashes, source references, version lineage and reproducible transformations.

Acceptance: Tests, human decisions, independent witnesses when needed, and an explicit publication or deployment gate.

An excellent agent may populate much of this automatically. It should not confer its own authority to destroy, disclose, publish or declare accepted. The better user experience is fewer manual controls because the underlying controls are reliably enforced, not because they were abandoned.

That is a meaningful new market for tools: an agent-native workbench that handles provenance and acceptance as routine infrastructure. It is more interesting than yet another chat interface wearing a project-management badge.

V. Software engineering is not dead. Its boundaries are changing.

Doshi makes a deliberately dramatic claim around 43:25: software engineering is being replaced by AI engineering. He describes both the anxiety experienced by career engineers and the historical possibility that a new technical platform rearranges which expertise carries economic advantage.

Read the statement as a challenge to an occupational boundary, not a measured obituary. Writing code is one activity within software engineering. The discipline also includes requirements, data modeling, failure analysis, security, safety, latency, architecture, integration, maintenance, operations and the choice of what not to automate. Generating a patch does not settle whether it is correct, maintainable, authorized or worth shipping.

A useful counterweight comes from the research organization METR. In an early-2025 randomized study of experienced open-source developers working on repositories they knew well, AI use increased measured task completion time by approximately 19% in that study. In a February 2026 update, METR observed possible improved outcomes in later data but said selection effects and measurement problems made the size of any gain uncertain. The original result is not a universal AI slowdown, and the later update does not establish a universal acceleration. Together they illustrate why felt productivity, benchmark success and accepted real-world productivity should be measured separately. METR 2025 study · METR February 2026 update.

The new opportunity is not the extinction of professionals. It is the migration of expertise toward problem framing, system composition, empirical evaluation, domain controls and accountability. Someone who once needed a full team to prototype an application may now build a credible first version alone. The requirement for experienced architects, security engineers, maintainers and independent acceptance may increase as the number of prototypes and production systems expands.

That is a different proposition from "everyone can ship anything." It respects both the expansion of capability and the persistence of consequences.

VI. GTA is an interesting benchmark precisely because it is not one benchmark

At 03:28, Doshi proposes a provocative sequence: measure how faithfully an AI system can rebuild successive Grand Theft Auto games, from the simpler early titles toward the enormous later releases. He observes that convincing demonstrations often precede production tools, and that complex games combine graphics, interactive systems, simulation, sound, story and code.

This is an idea for an evaluation, not a completed experiment. The interview does not show an agent rebuilding GTA, provide a protocol, establish a pass rate, or measure development costs. A single percentage called "accuracy" would be an inadequate metric for a large interactive product. One must distinguish visual resemblance, interactive behavior, content completeness, robustness under player actions, accessibility, performance and the quality of the created artifacts.

A credible research benchmark could use rights-cleared, incrementally complex reference games with known state transitions, asset requirements and user tests. It should measure playable functionality, regression stability, source and asset licensing, defect density, human acceptance and total project cost. A polished trailer and a functional game are different artifacts.

The useful idea here is not specifically GTA. It is the movement from static prompts and isolated benchmarks toward composed, sustained, multi-modal work. That could expose important limits that short benchmark questions miss. It could also tempt laboratories to optimize for a spectacular score that poorly represents valuable production work. A good benchmark should lead toward real acceptance, not become the next imitation target.

VII. When workers can do more, who is allowed to attempt more?

The latter part of the interview makes the economic argument personal. At 51:12, the speakers discuss how a person assigned one specialized operation might aspire to own a complete project. For the video worker who traditionally only adjusted color, the bigger question could be whether they can conceive, produce and deliver a film. The interviewer's own desire to move beyond short social clips toward films is an example of the motivation.

That is the interview's deepest insight. The point of cheaper execution is not necessarily to do an old job a little faster. It is to change the feasible set of projects someone can responsibly consider.

There is an important intellectual relationship to Paul Graham's 2023 essay How to Do Great Work, which Doshi mentions around 46:43. Graham emphasizes curiosity, experimentation and original problem selection. AI can lower the cost of pursuing a neglected question, but cannot supply interest, direction, independence of judgment or the willingness to abandon a bad approach.

Doshi is right to stress structural permission, too. A worker may have the ability to assemble a prototype but not permission to access production data, publish under a company's brand, commit organizational funds or displace another team's responsibility. A person with dependents, inflexible working hours, limited financial reserves or no reliable compute access does not enjoy the same freedom to experiment as a well-capitalized founder. Model access may reduce some inequalities while leaving others intact or even widening them.

If the opportunity is genuinely broad, its success should not be measured solely by the accomplishments of founders already able to spend thousands on AI subscriptions. Look at the median worker's ability to attempt useful projects, validate outcomes and keep the value created. Look also at who controls distribution, source data, inference infrastructure, intellectual property and permissions. Lower technical barriers are important, but they are not the same as equal bargaining power.

An organization serious about scaling ambition would permit bounded experimentation, give workers safe access to tools, define outcome-based review, make results portable, and reward useful projects rather than merely the quantity of artifacts produced. That is a management and governance redesign as much as an AI purchase.

VIII. Robotics is the most exciting extrapolation and the least proven

Near 56:12, the conversation turns to robotics. The speakers imagine a trajectory from agents that can move an arm or produce a drawing toward broader creative access to physical automation. The analogy to early game-generation demonstrations is vivid.

The distinction is that robots act in a world with friction, uncertain observations, people, equipment and consequences. Producing code that commands a robotic arm is not evidence of robust grasping, calibrated control, safe speed limits, recovery from unexpected contact, reliable sensing or validated operations. A one-off demo may be technically impressive without supporting an autonomous factory or consumer deployment.

A defensible evaluation would define physical tasks with real safety constraints, held-out environments, diverse objects and operators, failure modes, interventions, hardware maintenance and repeatability. Include simulator-to-real transfer, emergency-stop behavior and liability. Do not equate generating a motion plan with reliably completing the physical action.

Doshi's prediction that more people will be able to attempt robotics projects is a sensible research question. His timeline and implied pace of real-world deployment are speculative. Those two judgments can coexist.

IX. A GhostMesh test: from falling token costs to expanding feasible work

The interview gives us an unusually useful hypothesis to test across three kinds of work. The design below is a proposal, not a test that GhostMesh has executed.

Workload Accepted outcome What a cheap-model win must prove
Code and operations A patch with passing relevant tests, bounded permissions, correct rollback and independent review Equal acceptable release quality, lower total accepted-job cost, no hidden security/recovery debt
Document and creative production A source-linked artifact meeting layout, accessibility, factual and editorial constraints Blinded satisfaction plus measurable content fidelity, provenance and retained versions
Long-horizon mixed-agent work An artifact or analysis supported by a traceable evidence chain and explicit authorization Stable performance over time, controlled tool use, no unauthorized sends or destructive actions

Use representative cases rather than hand-picked successes; include edge cases and failure recovery. Pin models, prompts, allowed tools, context, cost assumptions and source states. Compare a premium model, at least one strong inexpensive model and a structured routing policy. Measure accepted outcomes, all attempts, tokens, tool charges, human verification minutes, latency, defects, privacy and rollback. Keep failures visible rather than quietly removing them from cost calculations.

Then add a second variable that most model benchmarks omit: scope expansion. Can a person complete a more ambitious project than before? Does the resulting quality increase? Is the project genuinely new rather than another variant of low-value output? Does another person accept it and find it useful? One might reasonably find that AI is most valuable not when the old task becomes 30× cheaper, but when a previously inaccessible project becomes affordable and safe enough to attempt.

This is the strongest falsifiable version of Doshi's opportunity thesis. If gains evaporate after source validation, retries, maintenance and human acceptance, then affordable tokens have not translated into accessible capability. If they survive those checks while expanding the class of work people can complete, the transformation is real.

X. The opposing argument deserves more than a footnote

There are at least five reasons to resist the interview's most enthusiastic reading.

First, capability is not fungible. A non-frontier model that performs acceptably on a familiar creative exercise may fail badly on an uncommon safety-critical or adversarial problem. The appropriate decision depends on failure severity and evidence, not mean preference scores.

Second, models do not make institutional authority disappear. Access controls, validated procedures, data provenance, confidentiality, contractual commitments and required reviewers are not merely irritating UI friction. Some are necessary safeguards. A headless agent still requires a defined authority boundary.

Third, productivity is hard to measure. A successful first draft can be thrilling while imposing substantial downstream rework. A model that feels faster can cost more time when experts integrate the output. METR's studies are reminders to measure whole-task impact with appropriate uncertainty, not proof that AI is incapable of accelerating work.

Fourth, lower prices can create dependence. The interview worries about concentrated pricing power while its proposed solution relies on a market with hardware bottlenecks, subsidies, capital-intensive training and vendor lock-in. Open weights increase options, but training, inference hosting, proprietary datasets and platform distribution can remain concentrated.

Fifth, economic opportunity is not evenly distributed. The ability to prototype new products does not itself confer customers, capital, organizational permission, reliable data, distribution or time. A reduction in technical barriers may produce greater creative participation, more competition and harsher market selection at the same time.

None of these objections cancels the larger possibility. They specify the conditions under which it should count as a genuine improvement.

The conclusion: scale the problem you can responsibly finish

The interview begins with a price comparison and ends with a question about ambition. That is an unusually revealing arc. Expensive models are not always worth their premium; cheap models are not always a bargain; and an agent that can produce files or code is not automatically authorized, accurate or finished.

The more important shift may be that the cost of trying is falling faster than the institutions around trying are changing. Once a single person can initiate more projects, the hard constraints move toward choosing worthwhile problems, judging outcomes, coordinating resources, maintaining state and deciding what deserves release. That is where both organizational advantage and organizational failure will accumulate.

Doshi's optimism is useful because it asks people to imagine bigger work rather than smaller invoices. It becomes more credible when coupled to disciplined experiments and explicit accountability. The right engineering response is not to defend every old workflow or celebrate its disappearance. It is to preserve the parts of the system that establish truth and authority while removing friction that no longer creates value.

GhostMesh takeaway: Treat intelligence as an increasingly available input, not as permission to abandon verification. A meaningful AI-native organization does not merely generate more. It can attempt more, prove more, retain more and responsibly accept more, with less human attention consumed by avoidable repetition.
