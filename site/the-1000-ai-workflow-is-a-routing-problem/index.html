The $1,000 AI Workflow Is Really a Routing Problem

David Ondrej’s three-day experiment is less convincing as a model leaderboard than as a demonstration of where expensive reasoning belongs, when to switch to cheaper execution, and why automation needs proof.

David Ondrej · November 12, 202630:41 videoOriginal GhostMesh analysis

David Ondrej’s three-day experiment is less convincing as a model leaderboard than as a demonstration of where expensive reasoning belongs, when to switch to cheaper execution, and why automation needs proof.

The short verdict: Copy the routing discipline. Do not copy the spending, agent count, or unsupported return-on-investment arithmetic.

What works? Use expensive reasoning when the problem is uncertain, faster execution when the work is bounded, another failure mode for review, and deterministic checks for known recurring work.

What remains unproven? That this model mix is best, that subscription costs accurately reflect the economics, or that a swarm of agents improves net delivery quality.

What should we test? Whether the workflow completes the same engineering tasks with fewer costly iterations and fewer escaped defects than a strong single-model baseline.

The full analysis

The impressive number is the least interesting part

David Ondrej opens his November 12 video with an arresting accounting claim: more than $1,000 of frontier-model usage in three days, more than 1,600 prompts, and substantial hands-on time. He also reports repeated usage-limit collisions. These figures describe the intensity of his experiment, not its effectiveness.

Prompt volume has no inherent relationship to useful output. A thousand prompts can represent disciplined exploration, a badly bounded problem, or an agent repeatedly revisiting its own mistakes. Subscription expenditure is equally ambiguous. It may include temporary usage allowances that do not resemble a direct API bill and may not survive a change in plan rules. Neither metric answers the question that matters: what did the work accomplish, with what human intervention, and what defect rate?

Once we put the headline aside, the video becomes much more useful.

Reason about the uncertainty, not every keystroke

Ondrej describes a progression across the three days. He initially leans heavily on his most expensive reasoning model, then delegates more work to faster models as he learns where the premium capability helps. His recurring examples are architecture, diagnosis, redesign, and difficult work with unknown failure modes.

That distinction is sound engineering judgment. A novel production failure may justify a model that spends more time developing hypotheses and interrogating assumptions. Once the failure is understood and the acceptance criteria are explicit, keeping the same costly model on every mechanical edit can be wasteful.

The unit of routing should be the uncertainty remaining in the task, not a permanent ranking of model brands. Complexity and uncertainty are related but not identical. A large, routine refactor may be simple to specify. A three-line authentication change may carry high consequence and hidden ambiguity.

The control point is a deliberate transition: state the diagnosis, freeze the intended change, define acceptance, and only then hand off bounded execution. Without that transition, a multi-model workflow merely adds a second opportunity for the task to drift.

[Source cues: 02:50 quota-limit claim; 02:31–07:00 usage patterns and allocation.]

Build reusable instructions only where repetition earns them

One of the less glamorous details is more transferable than the subscription strategy. Ondrej reports using Raycast snippets for a substantial share of his prompts and skills for larger recurring procedures. The point is not the snippet application. It is the elimination of repeatedly reconstructing operational context and instructions.

A reusable instruction earns its place when it reliably captures a task that recurs. A skill should encode actual procedure and boundaries, not become a ceremonial wrapper around a one-off request. The more elaborate the framework, the stronger the evidence needed that it reduces mistakes, time, or operator burden.

We should be skeptical of a system that needs an elaborate procedure to decide whether a simple procedure is worthwhile.

[Source cue: 04:01 Raycast snippets and reported prompt share.]

The strongest economic idea needs better measurement

Ondrej argues that cost per completed task matters more than nominal token price. Correct. A model that costs more per call could still be cheaper if it finds the defect in two attempts while a cheaper alternative spends fifteen attempts and consumes an engineer’s attention.

But a persuasive analogy is not a matched comparison. To demonstrate the claim, we need consistent task difficulty, equal acceptance criteria, wall-clock time, human review time, model usage, failures, and rework. If the expensive system happens to receive the hardest tasks, its apparent average cost may look worse while its marginal value is higher. If it receives the easiest tasks, the reverse happens.

The useful metric is not “how many hours agents worked” either. Parallel agent-hours can grow while human-ready output remains unchanged.

[Source cue: 11:13 cost per completed task.]

Different reviewers can disagree for different reasons

The video describes using a separate model family as a reviewer. There is a plausible advantage: models with different training, architectures, and tooling may catch different mistakes. That is a reason to test cross-family review, not proof of its independence.

A second model can share the first model’s blind spots, accept the same faulty premise, or simply produce another confident opinion. Review earns trust when it is asked to challenge specific claims, inspect actual changes, and validate against independent evidence. Tests, logs, source specifications, and observable behavior remain stronger than model-to-model agreement.

This matters even more when the creator describes shipping small changes directly to production. A change can be small in line count and large in impact. Authentication, authorization, payment, migration, and data-handling changes do not become safe because a model says the diff looks simple.

[Source cues: 13:01 Gemini review; 14:32 claimed productivity; 15:38 direct-to-production practice; 15:52 cross-family review.]

Ten agents do not equal ten independent engineers

The most theatrical passages show a premium planning model launching faster executor subagents, sometimes in large groups. Parallel exploration is valuable when tasks have separable boundaries and results can be reconciled. Otherwise it multiplies contention, duplicated investigations, contradictory edits, and review cost.

The right question is not “How many agents can we launch?” It is “How many independent work units can we define, validate, and integrate without multiplying risk?”

Role prompts intended to make an agent act as a co-founder or owner can change its conversational posture. They cannot grant authorization. The boundary between confidence and permission must remain explicit. A model encouraged to be proactive still needs approval for the actions that require it.

[Source cues: 19:37 ten Opus subagents; 23:51–24:51 model switching and owner/co-founder role framing.]

The best always-on agent may be a script

Near the end, Ondrej describes monitoring loops, some of which are scripts that invoke a model only when something anomalous occurs. That is a more mature architecture than an LLM continuously improvising its way through a known check.

Known inputs and known predicates usually favor deterministic code. The model earns its inference cost at the boundary: interpreting a novel signal, proposing a diagnosis, or explaining a conflict. This reduces cost, makes failure easier to reproduce, and keeps routine monitoring auditable.

His warning against letting standing loops invent new product features is worth retaining. Maintenance automation and product authority are not the same responsibility.

[Source cues: 27:21 35 reported loops; 27:30 script-only checks; 29:08 warning against novel feature invention.]

The model must also be allowed to see the data

There is a governance constraint missing from the video's spending calculus. Anthropic's current documentation lists Claude Fable 5.2 as subject to a 30-day data-retention requirement; access under zero-data-retention terms requires express authorization. A model may be capable of an engineering task and still be unsuitable for the repository, incident log, patient-related information, or regulated records involved.

The routing decision therefore begins before model quality: classify the data, confirm the applicable platform, contractual retention, and approved processing boundaries, and only then compare capability, latency, and cost. For regulated work, a faster or more capable answer from an unauthorized processing path is not a successful task.

Anthropic also confirms that Fable 5.2's effort control changes thinking depth, tool calls, latency, and token use, with uneven gains at the highest settings. This reinforces the need to calibrate on actual tasks rather than assume “maximum” is always more effective. Its documentation warns that inherited instructions to delegate can create excess cost or side effects. More agents are not a free quality upgrade.

[Vendor-primary sources: Anthropic Fable 5.2 model overview and prompting guide, linked below.]

What GhostMesh should copy

The practical experiment is small. Take eight to twelve representative engineering tasks from the existing backlog, with clear success criteria and comparable risk. Give half or matched pairs to a strong single-model baseline, and the others to a staged workflow: premium model for uncertainty and plan, faster model for bounded implementation, different-family review only where it adds a meaningful check.

Measure accepted outputs, time to validated completion, human review minutes, actual usage, retries, reverted changes, and escaped defects. Keep the same approval boundaries and existing execution surfaces. Use synthetic or otherwise authorized non-confidential tasks until the data-processing and retention terms for every selected model are verified. Record which step actually changed the outcome.

The result might favor the staged workflow for ambiguous tasks and the single model for straightforward ones. That is not a failure of the experiment. It is the routing rule we are trying to learn.

A mesh becomes stronger when it knows which work not to send to a model, which work does not justify multiple agents, and which production claims require more than a success message.

Bottom line: Buy reasoning only where it changes a decision. Keep execution bounded, review grounded in independent evidence, and routine automation boring enough to trust. The proof is the completed and verified task, not the size of the model bill.
