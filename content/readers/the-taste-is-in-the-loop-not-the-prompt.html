The Taste Is in the Loop, Not the Prompt

A longer prompt cannot compensate for an absent visual standard. Leon Lin's Cook method makes the standard visible, then asks an agent to keep measuring its distance from the target. The productive idea is the loop. The dangerous shortcut is believing its quality score.

In Creator Of Taste Skill Just Revealed His Entire AI Design Workflow, AI LABS walks through Leon Lin's design process, from reference gathering and mockups to criticism and a reusable agent skill. The tutorial asks a practical question: why do so many agent-built websites look interchangeable, even when the coding model is capable? Its answer is that the agent needs a better target and a sustained review process, not merely a longer instruction.

This Reader examines the demonstrated workflow, checks the publicly available Cook implementation, and separates its useful engineering discipline from speculative similarity percentages. It is about building a process that can notice bad design, not handing aesthetic authority to a number.

What the workflow actually changes

References turn taste into inspectable evidence

At 00:53, the presenter argues for supplying visual references rather than trying to describe every design property in a prompt. This is the strongest idea in the tutorial. A screenshot simultaneously specifies scale, hierarchy, whitespace, contrast, typography, component density, and spatial relationships. A sentence saying “make it modern” specifies none of those with comparable precision.

The recommendation is not to copy another product's screen. It is to identify what a reference does well and make the agent explain which properties it intends to transfer. A reference without an explicit interpretation can still produce imitation, incoherent mixtures, or an inaccessible interface.

Mock up the composition before spending effort on implementation

At 03:39, the sequence moves from inspiration to a target. The video's practical argument is that a mockup makes mismatch visible earlier than a complete coded page. That is plausible engineering economics, not a measured token-efficiency result. It depends on the complexity of the design, the fidelity of the mockup, and the number of iterations saved.

For a real design system, the target must include responsive states and the behavior of controls. A beautiful desktop reference is not enough to specify a 320-pixel layout, keyboard navigation, high text scaling, loading states, or failure recovery.

The 99% target is a forcing function, not a test result

The presenter discusses numerical quality targets around 04:58 and describes a lower observed assessment during an example. Those numbers invite a mistaken interpretation: that subjective visual similarity can be compared like a compiler test or pixel error rate.

The Cook rubric itself explicitly calls 99.9% a likely unreachable stretch target. It also distinguishes command-measured values, such as regional color differences, from human or model judgments about composition and materials. The latter remain ratings. Even a median of several fresh critics does not establish calibrated validity across designs, audiences, or accessibility needs. This is a good operational discipline precisely when the score is labeled honestly.

More iteration can improve an artifact without proving it was worth the cost

At 07:31, the tutorial asks the builder to give the process time. The Cook instructions formalize that with a minimum duration for ambitious demos and repeated rounds of stills, comparison, criticism, and repair. The accompanying README reports a particularly large build lasting roughly 45 hours and using millions of tokens. Those figures are creator-reported demonstration context, not a reproducible price/performance benchmark.

A time floor may counter premature declarations of completion, but it can also reward spending after marginal improvement has collapsed. For ordinary product work, a budget cap, visible quality gap, and owner's stop decision are better acceptance criteria than elapsed hours alone.

Specific criticism beats a universal “no slop” incantation

At 08:42, the presenter lists generic visual patterns that make generated pages feel alike. A ban list can help, but it is style-specific. Monospace, a serif heading, or purple may be wrong for one brand and exactly right for another. The underlying failure is unexamined repetition, not any one font or color.

The more generalizable advice arrives around 10:15: build and refine the piece in stages. Compare the actual output with the intended result, identify one precise defect, and make the next change testable. “Improve the page” is weak feedback. “The mobile heading clips at 200% text scaling” is a usable correction.

Fresh-context critics reduce self-congratulation, but are not automatically independent

At 11:51, the video advocates outside judgment. Cook's critic protocol calls for reviewers who see side-by-side outputs and a rubric without the builder's history or score. That reduces direct anchoring and supplies concrete revision targets.

It does not guarantee statistical independence. Reviewers using similar models, training data, or the same ambiguous rubric can share a systematic bias. Their median can be stable and wrong. A human owner, actual users, and task-based usability evidence remain distinct checks.

Cook is an operating loop, not a magic design model

At 12:53, the video introduces Cook. The repository contains a skill definition and references for intake, target and bar, visual comparison, critic scoring, rule setting, and shipping. It keeps a brief, reference shots, stills, comparison images, and a rounds ledger in the project. Its documented loop is build → render → compare → critique → fix.

That is an inspectable workflow specification. It does not by itself prove that running the skill yields beautiful, accessible, maintainable or cost-effective software. The distinction matters because a reusable procedure is easier to audit than taste, but it cannot eliminate judgment from its own exit condition.

What to adopt, and what to leave behind

01 / KEEP

Reference and comparison

Benefit

Comparable screenshots reveal concrete visual differences across revisions.

Limit

Examples must be interpreted, not copied; mobile and accessibility states need their own targets.

02 / ADAPT

Fresh-context critics

Benefit

Targeted criticism and a rounds ledger expose the remaining weaknesses.

Limit

Model agreement is not independent user research or acceptance.

03 / REJECT

Automatic numerical closure

Benefit

An aspirational bar discourages a first-pass declaration of success.

Limit

A subjective 99% score, or a mandatory duration, cannot replace risk-based acceptance and budget control.

For GhostMesh, the bounded experiment is one existing screen with owner-selected references, a responsive-state checklist, screenshot comparisons, actual accessibility checks, and a fixed iteration budget. Keep the changes that improve identifiable defects. Stop when further revisions no longer justify their cost, not when a model calls the work 99% complete.
