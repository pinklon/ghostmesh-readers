The Experiment Ran. Did the Research Advance?

An agent can run experiments, build code and write a paper. A research question asks whether that work changes what experts know. Two shadow evaluations show why the answers must stay separate.

01 / What was tested

They tested research questions, not just task completion.

The researchers took central questions from two unpublished AI papers. One concerned the structure and control of model personas; the other concerned detecting distribution shift in a tabular foundation model. The agents were asked to pursue each question without seeing the original authors' findings. The authors who had worked on those questions then reviewed the agent-written papers. That design gives the reviewers unusual subject expertise, while making the assessment non-blind.

In the main runs, the agents used a general-purpose scaffold with access to tools, a virtual machine, web resources and compute. The original deadline was 120 hours. The researchers granted a further 24 hours after draft papers had already been submitted. They also fixed a harness bug and requested clearer prose. The paper's account describes those as human interventions, so “without human help” needs that qualification.

02 / What the result bears

The work happened. The reviewers rejected the papers.

The study reports that the agents completed extensive research engineering: literature searches, code, GPU experiments, reviews and finished manuscripts. The original authors nevertheless scored the Personas paper 2 out of 6 and the TabPFN paper 1 out of 6. See the original study. They found the research choices and conclusions too weak for the conference standard they applied. The paper also reports a few minor results that interested the reviewers. Rejection should not erase the engineering, and engineering should not be renamed a research advance.

The useful distinction is between completing the research workflow and earning confidence in the answer.

The authors describe early commitment to weak approaches, insufficient response to critical reviews, poor backtracking, resource awareness problems and instruction drift. Those are their interpretations of these runs, supported by the materials they examined. GhostMesh has not independently reproduced the experiments or audited all trajectory logs. A second model and native scaffold produced similar failure patterns on one of the two questions; that is relevant to scaffold concerns but cannot establish a universal model limit.

03 / What keeps the claim bounded

Two cases cannot set a ceiling.

The two questions were selected, the grading was non-blind, and the original researchers spent much longer and used more GPU time than the agents. The authors argue that the agents left resources unused and that reviewers objected mainly to the quality of decisions, but unequal budgets still prevent a matched human performance claim. Future models, other fields and better scaffolds could behave differently.

RE-Bench reports strong agent performance in bounded, verifier-scored research engineering tasks. AI Scientist-v2 reports a workshop paper that crossed an acceptance threshold in its review setup. Both matter as context for what agents can do. Neither repeats these shadow questions or grades the same kind of research progress, so neither cancels this case study.

What remains open

  • Original-author reviews are expert and non-blind. The sample contains only two research questions.
  • The full agent paper and detailed logs for the unpublished TabPFN case are withheld. Personas code is public; its availability is not independent reproduction.
  • The arXiv version record dates v2 to August 7, 2026, while its experimental HTML title block says August 24. We cannot reconcile those dates from the available record.
  • Our source extraction is partial, especially for figures, tables, equations, OCR and reading order. Figures and experiments are not independently verified here.

04 / GhostMesh's call

Make research judgment an explicit gate.

This paper is worth studying if you are deciding what to delegate to research agents or how to evaluate them. It is a poor source for a sweeping prediction about when AI will automate all research. GhostMesh would use it to design a bounded review exercise: name the open question, require a reason for each experiment, ask what result would change course, bring an expert critique before the deadline, and record whether the agent revises the method or merely narrows its wording. That is a proposed evaluation, not a control or architecture change we have tested.

Skip the source if you need a simple victory or failure score. Read it if you need a sharper way to distinguish a productive research assistant from a system that can set direction and defend conclusions under expert scrutiny.
