A benchmark is not a lab: what one AI-biology trial actually measured

A 2025 physical-lab trial found no significant difference in novice completion of its pre-registered core task sequence with access to frontier AI. Its secondary measures show possible help at earlier steps, and a separate small lab pilot points toward uplift. GhostMesh reads these together as a measurement warning: digital capability, human progress, and completed physical work are different outcomes. The next useful evaluation must specify the user, model, tools, task, baseline, and safeguards before drawing a risk or safety conclusion.

Active Site and METR are the same trial family; LANL is a separate, small physical task; AISI reports different digital measures. No interview speech is used.

What this is about

The Active Site investigators tried to answer a question that biology benchmarks leave open: does access to a frontier language model help a novice finish physical laboratory work, compared with ordinary internet access? Between June and August 2025, they randomized 153 people with minimal lab experience to internet resources alone or the same resources plus a choice of then-current models from three vendors. Participants worked independently in a supplied lab across 39 sessions over eight weeks. The models were selected for relevant benchmark performance, and their safety classifiers were disabled in this study. That makes the result more informative about the offered capability than about a normal consumer account with safeguards, but it is still a result about these tools and this setting.

The experiment had a defined primary outcome. Each participant was assessed on whether they completed a sequence of three core tasks that modeled part of a larger biology workflow. It did not ask participants to assemble an unsupervised, end-to-end project or procure and set up their own lab. The distinction matters: a benchmark answer, a partial step, the study's composite completion, and a real-world project are four different claims.

Why it matters

AI-biology discussions often slide from a model scoring well on a digital test to an assumption that a novice can carry out difficult physical work. The UK AI Security Institute's frontier trends report documents substantial gains on biology questions and protocol evaluation, while explicitly separating those measures from human-uplift studies and saying that its report does not capture every factor in real-world impact. Active Site supplies the rarer human outcome. It should matter to safety evaluators, model developers, and readers weighing claims about biological misuse or beneficial lab assistance. It is worth reading for the contrast between final completion and partial progress. A reader seeking a current-model forecast or an account of Bill Gates's interview should look elsewhere.

What the trial found

On the pre-registered main outcome, four of 77 AI-assisted participants completed the core sequence, compared with five of 76 in the internet group: 5.2% versus 6.6%. The reported risk ratio was 0.79, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.24 to 2.62 and P = 0.759. The study did not detect an advantage on that endpoint. Its wide interval and small number of completions also leave room for meaningful benefit or harm; a null test is not a certificate that the true effect is zero.

The rest of the data resist a simple dismissal. In the full analysis, AI users had numerically higher success on four of five individual tasks, but no individual-task success difference was statistically significant. For one cell-culture task, the full analysis found 68.8% versus 55.3% success (P = 0.059). A secondary analysis restricted to 128 participants who met the attendance threshold found 79.7% versus 62.5% on that task (P = 0.025). That narrower result deserves attention, but it cannot be substituted for the randomized cohort's primary result. The investigators also measured earlier procedural progress and time to success. AI users tended to advance farther; a post-hoc pooled model estimated a risk ratio of 1.42 for a hypothetical typical task, with a 95% credible interval of 0.74 to 2.62. This is a model-dependent secondary estimate, not a measured completion rate for an integrated project.

The observed constraints are as instructive as the effect estimates. Participants used the AI heavily, yet the investigators report task-dependent errors and trouble getting accurate, specific answers. Both groups rated video demonstrations as particularly useful. Inference: the model can help someone find or navigate an early step while hands-on skill, equipment knowledge, and error recovery still govern completion. That inference is consistent with this trial; it is not a demonstrated universal barrier that future interfaces cannot cross.

What pushes against the easy reading

An independent Los Alamos National Laboratory pilot put ten lab novices into a different physical task with internet access or internet plus an AI model. On the first attempt, three of five AI participants and one of five internet participants completed its assigned task. The authors report four of five versus three of five after two attempts, although their narrative says only one additional internet participant completed on the second attempt, which does not reconcile with the reported three of five total. This is a directional counterpoint to the Active Site main result, not a replication or a precise effect estimate: its sample is tiny, its task and model differ, and experts offered limited help when participants became stuck. Its authors explicitly say it was not powered for a significant effect. Together the two experiments show why physical-lab testing matters, while neither settles the size of current biological uplift.

Digital evidence also pushes against treating the Active Site null as a lasting safety threshold. AISI reports improved model performance on biology knowledge, protocol, and troubleshooting evaluations. Some evaluated protocols were checked for feasibility in a wet lab. It also says internal novice studies show successful lab work with LLM access, but gives no matching sample, control result, or protocol in this report from which to estimate uplift. Those signals warrant follow-up; they do not measure the same published human outcome. A 2026 methods study based on interviews with 16 uplift-study practitioners describes how changing models, user skill, and baselines weaken the transport of one randomized result to another setting. Some of its authors overlap with Active Site, so it is methodological context rather than an independent validation of Active Site's effect estimate.

Active Site itself names the limits. Most participants were undergraduates; 128 of 153 met its per-protocol attendance threshold. The tasks were separate and some were simplified; material acquisition and infrastructure setup were excluded. The observed low base rate left the main outcome underpowered: the investigators' post-hoc calculation gave about 36% power to detect an odds ratio of 2 and 73% for an odds ratio of 3. The result does not estimate expert users, newer models, purpose-built scaffolds, or a complete unauthorized project. Nor does excluding procurement and setup mean those real-world barriers are easy. Their difficulty simply was not measured here.

GhostMesh's take

Inference and design rule: keep three levels of evidence separate: what a model can answer, what a person can accomplish with it during a task, and what that person can finish in a real setting. Do not promote a strong benchmark to a completed-project claim, and do not promote a flat composite outcome to a safety guarantee. For every consequential claim, record the actor's prior skill, model and access date, enabled tools and safeguards, comparator, physical task, final outcome, intermediate milestones, and uncertainty. Put the denominator next to every success percentage.

The next useful test is not another unqualified headline score. A prospective, ethically reviewed replication should specify a concrete and safe task, include a credible internet-only baseline, measure both final completion and points of failure, and state how assistance and safety intervention affect the comparison. Repeat it when model, interface, or user population materially changes. A METR collaborator's account says this trial cost $1.9 million and took a four-person team more than a year of dedicated work. That account is not an independent check on the trial's outcomes, but it makes a practical point: use repeat physical studies to calibrate cheaper digital evaluations between them, and state when a changed model or interface makes the calibration stale. This is an evaluation agenda, not a request to execute biology experiments in the Reader project.

What would change this conclusion: a larger, independently run physical-lab study with a newer specified system that shows a reliable difference on a pre-registered, consequential human outcome would raise or lower the estimated uplift for that setting. A well-measured lack of effect across multiple tasks, actors, and access conditions would narrow the concern. Neither a benchmark gain alone nor another small, ambiguous null would do that work. Gates's August 2026 essay offers a public argument for taking biological misuse seriously; it is same-speaker context for the separate held interview, not evidence about the trial or any words spoken in that episode.

Reading call: read the Active Site paper for its measured primary result, then read its individual-task and progress analyses with their secondary status in view. Use the LANL pilot as a different physical outcome and AISI as a digital capability signal. When a claim jumps between those levels, ask which human outcome would actually test it.
