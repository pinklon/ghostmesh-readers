# When Every AI Website Looks Good, What Makes One Actually Work?

**GhostMesh Reader candidate | Source-limited editorial draft | November 9, 2026**

Nate Herk has a sharper critique of AI-generated websites than the familiar complaint that they all look alike. The problem is not that code generators cannot make attractive pages. It is that visual fluency often conceals a missing argument. A visitor arrives with a question. The page responds with motion, gradients, slogans and cards. Neither has necessarily moved closer to an answer.

Herk's November 9 video, *I Studied 10,000 AI Websites… Here's what I learned*, offers five remedies: copy that mirrors the visitor, restrained accent colors, dimensional composition, animation with a purpose, and something useful to try before being asked to commit. The recommendations are practical. Their limits are just as instructive.

The most important distinction is between a page that persuades and a product that helps someone operate. A landing page may benefit from a single prominent action. Mission Control cannot turn every condition into a sales funnel. A reader needs an argument and its sources. An operator needs the state of a system, its consequences, its owner, and the next safe action.

## The brief

**What is the video about?** Herk argues that five recurring design decisions distinguish useful, credible AI-generated marketing sites from polished but generic output. He illustrates them with redesigns, website examples and reported conversion results. The headline's 10,000-site count is his assertion, not a disclosed reproducible sampling study.

**Why should readers care?** Coding agents make frontends cheap to produce, but cheap production does not decide what a visitor should understand, where attention should go, or whether an interaction is useful. Watch for a practical design critique. Treat marketing performance figures as leads for verification, not universal design laws.

**What does GhostMesh take away?** Adopt intent-first copy, semantic emphasis, functional depth, purposeful motion and interactive proof. Preserve explicit exceptions for high-density operational surfaces. Test comprehension and task completion alongside visual quality.

**Verdict:** Worth watching, especially the mirror test at [00:24](https://youtu.be/mImbbszCjCI?t=24), depth at [04:19](https://youtu.be/mImbbszCjCI?t=259), motion rules at [08:15](https://youtu.be/mImbbszCjCI?t=495), and interactive examples at [09:09](https://youtu.be/mImbbszCjCI?t=549). Do not import the color ratio or conversion numbers as unqualified standards.

## The mirror is a task, not a slogan

At [01:25](https://youtu.be/mImbbszCjCI?t=85), Herk describes a visitor recognizing their own problem and world in the opening screen. He reduces the test to three questions: who is this for, what do I get, and what do I do next. This is an excellent constraint on generic AI copy. The model may be able to describe a business eloquently without explaining the outcome its actual customers seek.

But recognition is not evidence of delivery. A headline can repeat a customer's language and still promise something the product cannot do. The right Design OS rule should combine intent-first presentation with honest capability. For an operational console, the user-centered first screen answers different questions: what changed, what matters, what happens if I ignore it, and what can I safely do?

Herk cites a trucking redesign reporting a [79.3% improvement](https://youtu.be/mImbbszCjCI?t=69), a City Click headline with a reported [90% pricing clickthrough increase](https://youtu.be/mImbbszCjCI?t=108), and a Campaign Monitor wording change with a [31.4% signup increase](https://youtu.be/mImbbszCjCI?t=116). Those are attributed marketing case examples. The caption readback does not establish their sample sizes, experiment design, confidence intervals, or transferability.

## A highlighter is useful because most of the page is not highlighted

At [02:44](https://youtu.be/mImbbszCjCI?t=164), Herk argues that one accent should identify the action requiring attention. He admits that his own first AI Automation Society redesign scattered blue across the page until the call to action lost its distinction. The self-critique is more valuable than a rigid palette recipe. It demonstrates that color becomes noise when it has no hierarchy.

He then borrows the interior-design [60/30/10 rule](https://youtu.be/mImbbszCjCI?t=209): most of the surface is background, a smaller portion provides structure, and the accent is scarce. As a quick marketing-page prompt, this may work. As a universal Design OS requirement, it fails. Mission Control may need separate, stable visual signals for risk, health, ownership, action and state. Reducing those meanings to one accent could make a console less understandable.

The transferable principle is an emphasis budget. Color must have consistent semantic meaning, adequate contrast, and redundant non-color cues. The question is not whether a page uses three colors or ten. It is whether users can tell what matters without deciphering a palette.

## Depth that conveys meaning

Herk compares a page with a stage: background establishes context, middle ground contains the content, and foreground identifies the action. At [05:08](https://youtu.be/mImbbszCjCI?t=308), he describes the AI Automation Society layered hero, including a foreground treeline that was adjusted to keep the action visible. He recommends an authentic background, a deliberate overlap, and elevation only for important items.

This connects directly to a criticism of many AI-generated dashboards: the interface becomes a wall of identical cards, each carrying similar borders and shadows, none explaining relative importance. Functional depth can help. A critical alert should feel closer than background telemetry. Selected content should stand apart from adjacent controls. Supporting detail should not compete with the primary question.

There is a limit. A layer without a hierarchy function is decoration. Overlap that looks striking in a desktop screenshot may obscure controls on a phone. Design OS should require explicit mobile composition and test actual viewport states, not extrapolate the desktop arrangement.

## Motion has to earn its place

At [06:54](https://youtu.be/mImbbszCjCI?t=414), Herk says an animation should show the product, direct attention, or explain what just occurred. At [08:14](https://youtu.be/mImbbszCjCI?t=494), he makes three practical recommendations: limit competing movement, display critical words immediately, and offer a reduced-motion path.

I agree strongly, with a contextual exception. A single moving object is a useful default for marketing composition, not a literal maximum for every application. Concurrent progress indicators may be legitimate in an operations dashboard, provided their motion is calm, comprehensible and not distracting. The real test is whether motion improves understanding of state and causality.

Herk cites Nielsen Norman Group on attention capture and a Purdue parallax study. Those citations deserve original-source review before we reproduce causal claims. His conclusion does not need exaggerated research: a moving effect without user benefit already fails a simple design critique.

Scrollcraft takes the craft further. Its creator-maintained repository separates a reusable scroll engine from each page's semantic markup, demands deliberate page grammar and a unique signature move, and verifies rendered frames rather than trusting a successful build. That separation of stable mechanism from creative expression is a better Design OS lesson than simply adding scroll animation. The project is built for marketing experiences; its specific device-count and emotional-peak rules should not be imposed on mission-critical consoles.

## The free sample should produce understanding

At [09:20](https://youtu.be/mImbbszCjCI?t=560), Herk proposes giving visitors a useful result before asking for a contact form. He describes a Canadian orthodontic price estimator and says that, **according to Outgrow, the calculator vendor**, it converted at approximately [45%](https://youtu.be/mImbbszCjCI?t=577). This is a reported conversion rate, not a 45% improvement. It is unrelated to the trucking example's 79.3% reported lift. The vendor attribution and missing denominator matter.

Herk invokes the IKEA effect to explain why a personalized result may feel more valuable. The psychological literature concerns effort and ownership; its application to every online calculator is an inference, not proof of conversion. An interactive feature that imposes effort without yielding useful information may be worse than a clear explanation.

For SharePlane and Readers, the strongest version of this pattern is not necessarily a quiz. It is interactive evidence: let a reader jump to the exact source passage, inspect competing claims, vary an architectural assumption, or see what changes under different constraints. For Hyperion Studio, interactive proof might be a qualified model comparison showing real output differences. For Mission Control, it means a clear explanation of an alert's consequence and available recovery action.

The design principle is utility before persuasion. Give someone a meaningful result, and be honest about its precision.

The practical heuristics can be evaluated as design proposals without pretending the numbers establish causality. ## What we would adopt

Use one existing screen per product: a Mission Control owner-attention view, Hyperion Studio's model-selection flow, a SharePlane article, and a Reader evidence panel. Record the user's principal question, the emphasis hierarchy, why each layer exists, the job of each animation, and the utility of each interaction. Compare before and after on mobile and desktop. Measure time to useful information, comprehension, task completion, errors, accessibility and perceived quality.

Keep the tests proportionate. This is not a license to construct another design-validation platform. A small set of real tasks, repeatable screenshots and a human review will tell us more than another pile of generated component screenshots.

The final lesson is not that AI websites need more decoration. It is that the interface must make a coherent promise, explain what matters, and help the person do something real. **Context endures. Possibility evolves. The user still has to understand what to do next.**
